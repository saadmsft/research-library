## Bottom line

::: caution Read this first
No study I found measured pas anfas by name. The two that come closest are small, have no control group, and cannot show that the breath practice caused the change. They are suggestive and nothing more.
:::

### The Indonesian dhikrullah study

Twenty participants practised three-minute sessions, five times a day for seven days, breathing 15 to 20 times a minute and saying "Allah" softly.[^pmc] Anxiety and cortisol both fell (p = 0.001). BDNF did not change significantly.

What this cannot tell you: with one group and no control, the fall could come from any of the following.

- Simply sitting quietly for three minutes five times a day.
- Expectation, and repeated measurement.
- Wudu. The participants' ablutions are a confounder in this design, because washing alone can settle people.

The pace matters too. Fifteen to twenty breaths a minute is within the ordinary adult resting range, so this was not slow breathing, and the result should not be read as support for claims about slow or held breathing.

### Aren and Tarlacı (2022)

A single-case QEEG study published in the *Journal of NeuroPhilosophy*. One person followed a ten-week breath and meditation practice, and EEG coherence increased.[^aren] The practice was not specifically pas anfas. A single case is a report, not a trial, and generalising from one person is not safe.

## What did not hold up

::: register Numbers and sources that circulate online
Anxiety and cortisol fell after a week of soft "Allah" breathing | verified | The Indonesian study, p = 0.001; single group, no control
BDNF changed | discarded | The same study reports it was not significant
EEG coherence rose after ten weeks of breath practice | verified | Aren and Tarlacı, a single case, not named pas anfas
An RCT found 42% versus 28% improvement on HAM-A | unverified | Cited as an IJSRT paper; I could not find or check it
A Springer review supports breath-based dhikr | unverified | I could not locate the review
Dhikr breathing raised HRV by 41% | discarded | ghayb.com cites a paper that does not support the figure
:::

The 42% against 28% figure and the Springer review are not claims I am rejecting. I simply could not find them, so they are out of the evidence until someone does.

## What would make a claim stronger

- A comparison group doing a similar silent sitting practice without the Name.
- Random assignment.
- Naming the practice exactly as taught, with the breath rate recorded.
- Following participants beyond one week.

## Safety

::: caution My reasoning, not a study finding
Nothing here tests forced breathing or retention. Those carry risks of dizziness, anxiety and fainting, and need more care with cardiac, respiratory, seizure or pregnancy conditions. If you have any of these, speak to a clinician before trying a breath practice. This is general reasoning and not medical advice.
:::
